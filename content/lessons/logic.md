# Follow what the premises actually allow

Draft editorial sample · Professional track · Version 0.1

This original teaching draft awaits independent correctness, originality and language review. Its invented premises teach formal reasoning; they are not factual claims about actual people or offices. Track placement and official coverage remain subject to verification.

## Learning objectives

- Distinguish a valid implication from its unsupported converse.
- Apply universal and existential statements without adding assumptions.
- Respect exclusive choices and ordering constraints as stated.

## Explanation

A valid conclusion follows from the given premises. It does not have to sound familiar, and a familiar statement is not automatically implied. Treat the premises as the rules of the exercise. Do not import unstated facts to rescue an answer.

“If a file is urgent, it receives a red tag” allows urgent implies red tag. It does not say that only urgent files receive red tags. A red tag alone therefore does not prove urgency. Under the stated rule, a file without a red tag cannot be urgent: otherwise it would have received the required tag. This is the contrapositive, not the converse.

Quantifiers also matter. “All approved records are signed” says something about every approved record. It does not, by itself, assert that any approved record exists. “Some approved records are archived” supplies existence: at least one approved record is archived. Combining these statements supports that some archived records are signed. It does not support that every archived record is approved.

In these exercises, or is inclusive unless the prompt explicitly states exactly one. “Exactly one of two boxes contains a card” supplies an exclusive condition. Do not invent exclusivity when the wording does not provide it.

Ordering questions require every stated constraint. If A is before B and B is before C, A is before C, regardless of whether the tasks are next to each other. Before does not necessarily mean immediately before.

## Worked example

Premises: All approved forms are signed. Some approved forms are archived. What follows?

**Some archived forms are signed.** The second statement supplies at least one form that is both approved and archived. The first statement makes that approved form signed. Nothing says that all archived forms are approved or that unsigned archived forms cannot exist.

## Practice

1. If every urgent file receives a red tag, does a red tag alone establish urgency? Explain.
2. If exactly one of boxes A and B contains a card, and A is empty, what follows about B?
3. A task list requires A before B and B before C. Must A and C be adjacent?

## Rationale

For item 1, no: another reason for a red tag has not been excluded. For item 2, B contains the card because the premises require exactly one of the two boxes to contain it. For item 3, A must precede C, but adjacency does not follow. Additional tasks could occur between them.

## Reflection

Label each step as a premise or an inference. When rejecting an option, try constructing a situation where all premises hold but that option is false. Independent review should check logical validity, existence assumptions and the exact meaning of every connector.
