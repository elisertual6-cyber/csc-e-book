# Read the labels before calculating

Draft editorial sample · Professional track · Version 0.1

This original teaching draft awaits independent correctness, originality and language review. Every table is invented for practice and is not a report of an actual government service. Official track coverage remains unverified.

## Learning objectives

- Read units, categories and time periods before using a table.
- Distinguish a total, a share, an average and a percentage change.
- State the limits of conclusions drawn from descriptive counts.

## Explanation

Begin with the title, row labels and units. A value of 40 could describe forms, people or minutes; calculations cannot supply a missing unit. Check whether rows represent separate groups that can be added or overlapping categories that would double-count the same observations.

A total adds compatible counts. An arithmetic mean divides that total by the number of observations, including genuine zero values. Do not treat a missing measurement as a measured zero. A category's share uses the relevant total as its denominator. A percentage change instead uses the starting value as its denominator; a change from 20 to 30 is 50 percent, while the reverse change is a decrease of about 33.3 percent.

A difference between two counts is not automatically a difference in efficiency. More completed forms might accompany more arrivals, longer opening hours or a different task mix. Without those denominators or a suitable study design, describe what the data show rather than claiming a cause or a performance ranking.

## Worked example

Invented counts of forms completed on three separate days:

| Day | Forms |
| --- | ---: |
| Monday | 24 |
| Tuesday | 30 |
| Wednesday | 36 |

The total is **24 + 30 + 36 = 90 forms**. The mean is **90 ÷ 3 = 30 forms per day** over these three days. Wednesday accounts for **36 ÷ 90 = 40 percent** of this total. The increase from Monday to Tuesday is **(30 − 24) ÷ 24 = 25 percent**. These calculations use different denominators because they answer different questions.

## Practice

Use the same invented table:

1. How many more forms were completed on Wednesday than on Monday?
2. What share of the three-day total was completed on Tuesday?
3. Does the table prove that Wednesday staff worked more efficiently? Identify missing information.

## Rationale

Item 1 is 12 forms. Item 2 is 30 of 90, or one-third. For item 3, no efficiency conclusion is established. Counts alone do not state staff hours, number of arrivals, task difficulty or resources. A higher output count is a descriptive result, not proof of a particular cause.

## Reflection

Write the unit beside every result and name the denominator before dividing. Independent review should recalculate values, verify rounding instructions and check that every inference respects the table's scope.
