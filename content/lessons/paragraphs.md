# Give each sentence a clear place

Draft editorial sample · Common curriculum · Version 0.1

This original teaching draft awaits independent correctness, originality and language review. It is an ungraded preview, not an official CSC item or an approved assessment.

## Learning objectives

- Identify a paragraph's controlling idea and relevant supporting details.
- Use time, cause and reference clues to arrange sentences.
- Distinguish a supported conclusion from an unrelated or exaggerated claim.

## Explanation

A coherent paragraph develops one controlling idea. Its sentences may describe, explain, compare or narrate, but each should contribute to the paragraph's purpose. A topic sentence states the idea; details support or illustrate it. The topic sentence does not have to come first in every kind of writing. In a short exercise, choose the location supported by the actual sentences rather than applying a position rule mechanically.

When arranging sentences, look for dependencies. A pronoun such as this may need an earlier idea to refer to. First, afterward and finally can indicate sequence. A proposed solution normally follows a description of the problem it addresses. However, a transition is evidence, not a complete ordering algorithm: two sentences can both begin with then while the actions themselves determine which comes first.

Try a tentative order and reread the whole paragraph. Does each reference have an identifiable antecedent? Can every action happen at that stage? Are time markers consistent? When more than one order is defensible, the question needs revision rather than an invented rule that makes one order seem compulsory.

A concluding sentence should fit the support already supplied. If a paragraph describes improvements in one room, a conclusion about every office in the country goes beyond the evidence. Likewise, a detail can be true in general yet irrelevant to the paragraph's specific purpose.

## Worked example

Arrange these instructions:

- A. Finally, seal the labeled parcel.
- B. First, place the book inside the box.
- C. Next, fill the gaps around the book with paper.
- D. Then close the box and attach its label.

The supported order is **B–C–D–A**. The book must be in the box before the gaps around it can be filled. The box is then closed and labeled. Finally, the labeled parcel is sealed. Both the sequence words and the action dependencies support this order. An ordering answer should explain those dependencies, not merely list letters.

## Practice

1. Write a topic sentence for these details: the room has quiet tables, reading lamps and sockets for laptops. Keep your claim within those details.
2. Add a relevant supporting sentence to your topic sentence. Then write an unrelated sentence and explain why it does not belong.
3. Revise this order: “This change reduced confusion. The team replaced the unclear signs.” Explain the reference that guides the revision.

## Rationale

For item 1, “The reading room provides several features for focused study” fits the supplied details. “The room guarantees higher examination scores” does not: no score evidence is provided. Item 2 has many possible answers, but the relevant sentence must support the same controlling idea. A sentence about distant ocean currents would normally be unrelated here.

For item 3, put the replacement first: “The team replaced the unclear signs. This change reduced confusion.” This change then has a clear preceding referent. The exercise supplies the reduction as a statement; it does not independently prove the causal claim in a real-world study.

## Reflection

After arranging a paragraph, explain the relationship between each adjacent pair of sentences. Independent review should test alternative orders and remove ambiguity before an item can be approved.
