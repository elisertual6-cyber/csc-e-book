# Look for the rule, then check it

Draft editorial lesson · Common curriculum · Version 0.1

Original teaching material awaiting independent review. Sequence examples are ungraded mathematical exercises, not actual CSC items or confirmed exam specifications.

## Learning objectives

- Test arithmetic and geometric rules against every displayed transition.
- Recognize a stated alternating or repeating pattern.
- Explain why a finite list alone does not determine a unique next term.

## Explanation

A sequence is an ordered list. In an **arithmetic sequence**, the difference between consecutive terms is constant. If the terms are 5, 9, 13 and 17, every difference is +4. Continuing that stated arithmetic rule gives 21.

In a **geometric sequence**, each term is multiplied by the same factor. For 3, 6, 12 and 24, the factor is 2, so the next term is 48. When checking a ratio, avoid dividing by zero; a zero term needs separate consideration. A constant difference and a constant factor are different kinds of rule.

Some exercises state a repeating operation cycle, such as “add 3, then multiply by 2.” Track which operation comes next. Other exercises may state that odd-position terms and even-position terms form separate sequences. Use the stated structure rather than changing rules halfway through to fit one convenient step.

A short finite list can be continued in many mathematically valid ways. If a task gives no rule, the next number is not uniquely determined just by the list. In these teaching examples, the arithmetic, geometric or alternating assumption is explicitly stated. Checking a plausible pattern is useful; claiming it is the only possible pattern is a stronger and often unjustified conclusion.

## Worked example

An exercise states: “Start at 4 and repeatedly add 3, then multiply by 2.” The displayed terms are 4, 7, 14, 17, 34. Find the next two terms under that rule.

1. 4 + 3 = 7; 7 × 2 = 14.
2. 14 + 3 = 17; 17 × 2 = 34.
3. The next operation is +3, so 34 + 3 = **37**.
4. Then multiply by 2: 37 × 2 = **74**.

Check the complete alternating cycle. Adding 3 twice would ignore the instruction even if the first new term looked reasonable.

## Practice

Use only the rule stated in each exercise. Write the differences, factors or operation order beside the terms.

1. Continue the arithmetic sequence 7, 12, 17, 22 by one term.
2. Continue the geometric sequence 81, 27, 9, 3 by one term.
3. Starting at 2, repeatedly multiply by 3, then subtract 1. The terms shown are 2, 6, 5, 15, 14. Find the next two terms.
4. Does the list 1, 2, 4 alone prove that 8 must be next? Explain briefly.

## Rationale

1. **27.** The common difference is +5, so add 5 to 22.
2. **1.** Each term is multiplied by 1/3, equivalently divided by 3. Apply the same factor to 3.
3. **42, then 41.** After subtracting 1 to obtain 14, the cycle returns to multiplication: 14 × 3 = 42, followed by 42 − 1 = 41.
4. **No.** A doubling rule would give 8, while a rule that adds consecutive integers gives +1, +2, +3 and therefore 7. Both match the three displayed terms. More information is needed for a unique answer.

## Reflection

Check every transition, not just the last pair. State the assumption used in your answer. A well-explained rule is more informative than a guessed number.
