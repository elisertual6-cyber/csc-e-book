# Support the answer with the passage

Draft editorial sample · Common curriculum · Version 0.1

This original teaching draft awaits independent correctness, originality and language review. All passages are invented teaching examples, not reports of actual government programs or official CSC examination material.

## Learning objectives

- Separate an explicit detail, a supported inference and an unsupported claim.
- Identify the main purpose and scope of a short passage.
- Preserve qualifications such as may, some and during the pilot when answering.

## Explanation

Begin with the question, then read the passage as a whole. Identify what the passage says before adding what you already believe about the topic. An explicit-detail question asks for information directly stated. An inference question asks for a conclusion supported by that information, even if it is not expressed in the same words. Neither kind permits an answer contradicted by the text.

Watch scope and certainty. Some does not mean all; a trial at one location does not automatically establish a nationwide result. May extend is a possibility, not a promise. A paragraph can report that two events happened together without proving that one caused the other. Good reasoning keeps these limits instead of replacing cautious language with a stronger claim.

For a main-purpose question, choose a statement broad enough to cover the important sentences but specific enough to reflect their actual work. A passage describing a trial and its measurement plan is not necessarily arguing that the trial has already succeeded. A title or summary should likewise represent the passage rather than advertise an outcome it has not established.

When two options look plausible, locate the words each would require. One may match an explicit detail, while the other adds an unstated date, cause, guarantee or universal claim. If the passage genuinely leaves a matter unresolved, the appropriate conclusion is that the information is insufficient, not that any guess is equally supported.

## Worked example

“A reading center will test evening opening for six weeks. Staff will record attendance during the trial. The center may continue the evening hours if demand is sufficient.”

Can we conclude that evening opening is permanent? **No.** The passage describes a six-week trial and makes continuation conditional on demand. Recording attendance supplies evidence for a later decision; it does not announce that decision. A supported summary is: “The center is testing evening hours before deciding whether to continue them.”

## Practice

Read this invented passage:

“During a five-day trial, a help desk recorded morning waiting times. On Friday, it opened a second counter. Staff will compare the five days before deciding whether to keep that counter.”

1. On which day did the second counter open?
2. Why are staff comparing the recorded waiting times?
3. Does the passage prove that a second counter always reduces waiting time? Explain the limit.

## Rationale

Item 1 is Friday, an explicit detail. For item 2, the comparison informs whether to keep the additional counter. The passage does not provide measured results or promise a particular decision.

For item 3, no such universal conclusion is supported. We have a short trial, no waiting-time values and no account of other factors such as arrival numbers. Even a lower Friday waiting time alone would not establish an always-effective causal rule. A useful answer identifies the missing evidence without pretending that the opposite conclusion has been proved.

## Reflection

For each answer, write either the supporting phrase or a short chain of reasoning from the text. Mark any qualification your answer must preserve. Independent review should verify that a question can be answered from its own passage and that exactly one offered option meets the stated task.
