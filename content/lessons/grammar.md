# Find the subject before choosing the verb

Draft editorial sample · Common curriculum · Version 0.1

This original teaching draft awaits independent correctness, originality and language review. Examples target common conventions of formal edited English; they are not official CSC items or judgments about every dialect.

## Learning objectives

- Identify a sentence's subject and match its verb in number.
- Use time clues to choose a consistent verb form.
- Check count nouns and distinguish a complete sentence from a fragment.

## Explanation

Find the subject before choosing the verb. In “The box of forms is ready,” box is the singular subject. Forms belongs to the phrase “of forms”; its position near the verb does not make it the subject. Compare “The boxes of forms are ready.” The changed subject requires a plural verb.

Each normally takes a singular verb in the formal pattern used here: “Each of the forms is numbered.” Two subjects joined by and usually take a plural verb: “The writer and the reviewer are responsible.” These are starting patterns, not a complete account of agreement; collective nouns and special constructions need separate treatment.

Time clues also matter. “Yesterday, the clerk reviewed the file” uses a completed past action. “The clerk reviews files each morning” describes a present routine. Do not choose a tense merely because the event sounds important. Read the stated timeline.

For quantities in formal prose, fewer commonly modifies plural count nouns, while less commonly modifies uncountable quantities. Compare fewer errors with less noise. Avoid presenting this as an exception-free rule for every expression of time, money or measurement.

A complete declarative sentence generally needs an independent clause. “Because the office closed early” leaves the reader waiting for the main clause. “We returned the next morning because the office closed early” completes the thought.

## Worked example

Choose a verb: “Each of the application forms ___ numbered before distribution.”

The subject is each, not forms. For the present-tense statement, **is** agrees with each: “Each of the application forms is numbered before distribution.” Choosing are would incorrectly use the nearby plural noun as the subject. A correct answer must fit the whole verb phrase, not just sound familiar in isolation.

## Practice

1. Complete: “The folders beside the printer ___ ready for collection.” Use is or are and identify the subject.
2. Complete: “Yesterday, the assistant ___ the totals.” Use checks or checked and explain the time clue.
3. Complete: “The revised draft contains ___ spelling errors.” Use fewer or less.
4. Turn “Although the meeting ended late” into a complete sentence.

## Rationale

Item 1 takes are because folders is plural. Item 2 takes checked because yesterday locates the completed action in the past. Item 3 takes fewer because errors is a plural count noun. Item 4 needs a main clause, such as “Although the meeting ended late, the team completed its notes.” Several completions are possible if the result is grammatical and coherent.

## Reflection

When revising, circle the subject, underline the complete verb phrase and mark any time clue. Explain a correction with a rule and context. Independent review should check both the target convention and whether another offered answer could also be defensible.
