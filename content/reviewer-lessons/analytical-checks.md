# Test a conclusion before accepting it

Draft editorial supplement. Original, unreviewed teaching material with proposed solutions. Premises, organizations and figures are invented for practice.

## Learning objectives

- Distinguish a one-way implication from its reverse.
- Test an unsupported conclusion with a counterexample.
- Compare completion rates using each group's own denominator.

## Explanation

A conclusion must follow from the stated premises. To test a proposed conclusion, look for a situation where every premise is true but that conclusion is false. One such counterexample is enough to show that the conclusion is not forced.

For numerical comparisons, preserve what a ratio measures. A larger completed count and a larger completion percentage are different claims. Identify both the numerator and the denominator before comparing groups.

## Worked example

### Example 1: A permit does not reverse the rule

Premise: “Every registered shuttle has a permit.” Vehicle P has a permit. Must Vehicle P be a registered shuttle?

1. The premise runs from registered shuttle to permit holder.
2. It does not say that only registered shuttles can hold permits.
3. Imagine that P is a permitted private vehicle rather than a registered shuttle.
4. The premise and P's permit can both be true in that example.

The proposed answer is **No; the conclusion is not established by the premise**. A permit is necessary for the stated shuttle group, but the premise does not make it sufficient for membership.

### Example 2: More completed items versus a higher rate

Team A completes 15 of 20 assigned checks. Team B completes 12 of 15. Which team has the higher completion rate?

1. Team A: 15 ÷ 20 = 0.75 = 75%.
2. Team B: 12 ÷ 15 = 0.80 = 80%.
3. **Team B has the higher rate**, although Team A completes more items in total.
4. Cross-check without decimals: 15 × 15 = 225, while 12 × 20 = 240. The second fraction is larger.

Neither calculation proves that one team's work was harder or more accurate. Those claims would need other evidence.

## Practice

Use only these premises: “Some editors are reviewers. All reviewers completed the training.” Which conclusion must follow?

A. All editors completed the training.
B. Some editors completed the training.
C. No editors completed the training.
D. Every trained person is an editor.

Draw an example with one person in both the editor and reviewer groups. Consider whether the premises say anything about every other editor.

## Rationale

The proposed answer is **B — Some editors completed the training**. The editors identified by “some” are reviewers, and all reviewers completed the training. Nothing states that every editor is a reviewer, so A is too broad. C contradicts the known trained editor-reviewers. D reverses and expands the given relationships.

## Common mistake

“All A are B” does not mean “all B are A.” Also, a claim about some members does not automatically extend to every member of their group.
